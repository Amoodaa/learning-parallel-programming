Philosophers and Readers/Writers

Two classic exam problems, both solved with lesson 12's lock and conditions. Philosophers can deadlock; readers can share; writers need the data to themselves.

Your win today: you'll write a readers/writers control that lets many readers in at once but never lets a writer overlap anyone. About 25 minutes.

Deck 4 slides 17–39 apply lesson 12's pattern to the two problems slide 4 promised. The dining philosophers bring back deadlock (lessons 3 and 11) with a new fix: limit how many threads can try. Readers/writers shows that "one thread at a time" is sometimes too strict.

1 · The dining philosophers

Deck 4 · slides 17–22

Five philosophers sit at a round table with one chopstick between each pair of neighbours (slide 17). A philosopher alternates between thinking and eating (slide 18), and to eat needs both the left and the right chopstick. Each philosopher is a thread (slide 22):

while (true) {
    think(phil);     // sleep 5 s  (slide 20)
    // pick up chopsticks
    eat(phil);       // sleep 3 s  (slide 21)
    // put down chopsticks
}

With 5 chopsticks and 2 needed each, at most 2 can eat at once (slide 19).

2 · Chopsticks, and the deadlock

Deck 4 · slides 23–27

A chopstick is a one-user resource, written like lesson 12's garden with a maximum of 1 (slides 23–25). pickUp() waits on notInUse while inuse is true, then sets it. putDown() clears it and signals. Slide 26 gives each philosopher its left and right chopstick:

ch1.pickUp(); ch2.pickUp();   // left, then right
eat(phil);
ch1.putDown(); ch2.putDown();

Slide 27: if all five pick up their left chopstick at the same moment, each then waits forever for the right one, which a neighbour is holding. All four Coffman conditions (lesson 3) hold:

  • Mutual exclusion: a chopstick has one user at a time.
  • Hold and wait: each philosopher holds the left chopstick while waiting for the right.
  • No pre-emption: nobody takes a chopstick out of another's hand.
  • Circular wait: 0 waits for 1, 1 for 2, and so on, and 4 waits for 0.

3 · The fix: at most n−1 at the table

Deck 4 · slides 27–31

Slide 27's fix: let at most n − 1 philosophers try to eat at once. DeadLockPrevention (slides 28–30) is lesson 12's garden again, with max = n − 1. enters() waits while eating == max, and leaves() decrements and signals. Slide 31 wraps the chopstick code in it:

deadLock.enters();            // at most 4 of 5 get past
ch1.pickUp(); ch2.pickUp();
eat(phil);
ch1.putDown(); ch2.putDown();
deadLock.leaves();

Why does it work? With 4 philosophers and 5 chopsticks, even if each of the 4 holds one chopstick, one chopstick is still free. That chopstick lies next to someone who is already holding their other one, so that philosopher can eat, finish and put both down. The circle can never close, so this breaks circular wait.

Two notes on slides 27–31. Slide 27 says the limit also stops a philosopher "eating forever". It doesn't: the limit counts philosophers, not time. What stops it is that eat() sleeps for a fixed 3 s. Also, volatile on slide 28's fields does no harm but isn't needed, because they're only used under the lock (compare Deck 3 s94–97 in lesson 11).

Slide 19 mentions another fix in words only: pick up both chopsticks at once (wait until both are free, then take both). That breaks hold and wait. Lesson 11's resource ordering also works: if every philosopher picks up the lower-numbered chopstick first, the cycle can't form.

4 · Readers/writers

Deck 4 · slides 32–39

Slide 32's rules for a shared data set:

  • only one writer at a time;
  • readers are blocked while a writer writes, and writers are blocked while anyone reads;
  • many readers at once are fine, because reading changes nothing;
  • writers take precedence over readers.

A plain lock would also be correct, but it would force readers to take turns for no reason. The control in slides 33–37 keeps three variables under one lock: readers (reading now), writing, and waitingWriters. It has two conditions: writers wait on wCond and readers on rCond.

MethodWaits whileThen
startWrite (s34)writing || readers > 0, counting itself in waitingWriterswriting = true
stopWrite (s35)never waitswriting = false; if a writer waits, wCond.signal(), else rCond.signalAll()
startRead (s36)writing || waitingWriters > 0readers++
stopRead (s37)never waitsreaders--; the last one out does wCond.signal()

Three details to understand:

  • Writer precedence comes from startRead also waiting while waitingWriters > 0. A new reader can't slip in while a writer is queued. The price: if writers keep arriving, readers can starve.
  • signalAll for readers. After a write, every waiting reader may go in together, so wake them all. With signal(), only one reader would wake, and nothing would ever wake the rest.
  • signal for writers. Only one writer can go in, so waking one is enough.

Slides 38–39 wrap it around an array. read(k) calls startRead() and returns, and the caller must later call stopRead() itself. That's fragile: forget it once and writers wait forever. It's safer to keep start, access and stop together in one method, with the stop in a finally as in lesson 11. (Slide 33 also leaves out a type: private lock should be private Lock lock.)

Check yourself

Answer from memory, without scrolling up. You get one click per question.

All five philosophers hold their left chopstick and wait for the right. Which Coffman condition does "at most n−1 may try" break?

With only 4 trying, one chopstick is always spare, so someone can complete a pair and the cycle never closes. Philosophers still hold one chopstick while waiting, so hold and wait is still there.

In readers/writers, what gives writers precedence over readers?

A queued writer blocks new readers, so the current readers drain away and the writer gets in. stopWrite also prefers waking a writer over the readers.

Why does stopWrite wake readers with signalAll(), but writers with signal()?

Waking one reader would leave the others asleep even though they're allowed in, and nothing else would wake them. Waking all writers would be wasted, because all but one would just wait again.

Why not protect the shared data with a single ReentrantLock for both reads and writes?

One lock is correct (lesson 8) but too strict. Reads don't change anything, so letting them overlap is safe and allows more concurrency. That's the same trade-off as coarse vs fine-grained locking in lesson 10.

Stuck? Hint

Every method is lock.lock(); try { … } finally { lock.unlock(); }. Use the table in section 4: the "waits while" column becomes a while loop around await() on the method's own condition. In startWrite, put waitingWriters++ just before the await() and waitingWriters-- just after it.

Stretch: break it on purpose

(a) In stopWrite, change rCond.signalAll() to rCond.signal(). What happens, and why? (b) Delete || waitingWriters > 0 from startRead. Does it still pass? Which rule from slide 32 did you drop?

Solution (only after trying) 
void startRead() {
    lock.lock();
    try {
        while (writing || waitingWriters > 0) {
            try { rCond.await(); }
            catch (InterruptedException e) {}
        }
        readers++;
    } finally { lock.unlock(); }
}
void stopRead() {
    lock.lock();
    try {
        readers--;
        if (readers == 0) wCond.signal();
    } finally { lock.unlock(); }
}
void startWrite() {
    lock.lock();
    try {
        while (writing || readers > 0) {
            waitingWriters++;
            try { wCond.await(); }
            catch (InterruptedException e) {}
            waitingWriters--;
        }
        writing = true;
    } finally { lock.unlock(); }
}
void stopWrite() {
    lock.lock();
    try {
        writing = false;
        if (waitingWriters > 0) wCond.signal();
        else rCond.signalAll();
    } finally { lock.unlock(); }
}

(a) It hangs (3 runs out of 3 on my machine): one reader wakes, and the others wait on rCond with nobody left to signal it. (b) It still passes, because no rule the checker tests is broken. You dropped "writers take precedence", so a steady stream of readers could now keep a writer out forever.

Exam practice

Describe the dining philosophers problem. Explain how deadlock can occur, and one way of preventing it. (6 marks)

Five philosophers sit at a round table, alternately thinking and eating, with one chopstick between each pair. To eat, a philosopher needs both adjacent chopsticks. Each chopstick can be used by one philosopher at a time. If every philosopher picks up their left chopstick at the same time, each waits forever for the right one, which their neighbour holds. All four conditions hold: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no pre-emption, and a circular wait around the table. Prevention: allow at most n−1 philosophers to try to eat at once, using a counter guarded by a lock and condition. With n−1 philosophers and n chopsticks, at least one philosopher can always get both chopsticks, so the circular wait can't form. (Other answers: pick up both chopsticks at once, which breaks hold and wait; or pick up the lower-numbered one first, which breaks circular wait.)

State the readers/writers rules and explain why multiple readers are allowed. (4 marks)

One writer at a time; no readers while a writer writes; no writer while anyone reads; any number of readers together; and, in the slides' version, writers before readers. Readers are allowed together because reading doesn't change the data, so overlapping reads can't interfere. Only a write conflicts with another access.

Marks are my estimate, not taken from your exam.

Why can the n−1 limit never deadlock five philosophers?

With 4 trying and 5 chopsticks, one chopstick is always free, so at least one philosopher can complete a pair, eat and release. The circular wait can't close.

Readers/writers: who does stopWrite wake, and how?

One waiting writer with signal() if there is one, otherwise all waiting readers with signalAll().

What are the four Coffman conditions? (lesson 3)

Mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no pre-emption, circular wait. All four are needed for deadlock.
